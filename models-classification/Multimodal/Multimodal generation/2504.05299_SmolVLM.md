###### Abstract

Large Vision-Language Models (VLMs) deliver exceptional performance but require significant computational resources, limiting their deployment on mobile and edge devices. Smaller VLMs typically mirror design choices of larger models, such as extensive image tokenization, leading to inefficient GPU memory usage and constrained practicality for on-device applications.

We introduce SmolVLM, a series of compact multimodal models specifically engineered for resource-efficient inference. We systematically explore architectural configurations, tokenization strategies, and data curation optimized for low computational overhead. Through this, we identify key design choices that yield substantial performance gains on image and video tasks with minimal memory footprints.

Our smallest model, SmolVLM-256M, uses less than 1GB GPU memory during inference and outperforms the 300-times larger Idefics-80B model, despite an 18-month development gap. Our largest model, at 2.2B parameters, rivals state-of-the-art VLMs consuming twice the GPU memory. SmolVLM models extend beyond static images, demonstrating robust video comprehension capabilities.

Our results emphasize that strategic architectural optimizations, aggressive yet efficient tokenization, and carefully curated training data significantly enhance multimodal performance, facilitating practical, energy-efficient deployments at significantly smaller scales.

1 Introduction
--------------

Vision-Language Models (VLMs) have rapidly advanced in capability and adoption(Achiam et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib1); Bai et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib7); Beyer et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib9); Chen et al., [2024c](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib16); McKinzie et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib65)), driving breakthroughs in cross-modal reasoning(Liu et al., [2024a](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib50), [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib49)) and document understanding(Appalaraju et al., [2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib6); Faysse et al., [2024a](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib22); Livathinos et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib57); Nassar et al., [2025a](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib66)). However, these improvements typically entail large parameter counts and high computational demands.

Since early large-scale VLMs like Flamingo(Alayrac et al., [2022a](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib2)) and Idefics(Laurençon et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib37)) demonstrated capabilities with 80 80 80 80 B parameters, new models have slowly appeared at smaller sizes. However, these models often retain high memory demands due to architectural decisions made for their larger counterparts. For instance, Qwen2-VL(Wang et al., [2024a](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib82)) and InternVL 2.5(Chen et al., [2024b](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib15)) offer smaller variants (1 1 1 1 B-2 2 2 2 B), but retain significant computational overhead. Conversely, models from Meta(Dubey et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib20)) and Google (Gemma 3) reserve vision capabilities for large-scale models. Even PaliGemma(Beyer et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib9)), initially efficiency-focused, scaled up significantly in its second release(Steiner et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib78)). In contrast, Moondream(Korrapati, [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib36)) keeps focusing on improving performance while maintaining efficiency, and H 2 2 2 2 OVL-Mississippi(Galib et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib25)) explicitly targets on-device deployment. Efficient processing is particularly critical for video understanding tasks, exemplified by Apollo (Zohar et al., [2024b](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib98)), where memory management is essential. Furthermore, reasoning LLMs generate more tokens during inference, compounding computational costs (DeepSeek-AI, [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib17); OpenAI et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib68)). Therefore, efficiency per token becomes vital to ensure models remain practical for real-world use. Our contributions are:

*   •Compact yet Powerful Models: We introduce SmolVLM, a family of powerful small-scale multimodal models, demonstrating that careful architectural design can substantially reduce resource requirements without sacrificing capability. 
*   •Efficient GPU Memory Usage: Our smallest model runs inference using less than 1GB GPU RAM, significantly lowering the barrier to on-device deployment. 
*   •Systematic Architectural Exploration: We comprehensively investigate the impact of architectural choices, including encoder-LM parameter balance, tokenization methods, positional encoding, and training data composition, identifying critical factors that maximize performance in compact VLMs. 
*   •Robust Video Understanding on Edge Devices: We demonstrate that SmolVLM models generalize effectively to video tasks, achieving competitive scores on challenging benchmarks like Video-MME, highlighting their suitability for diverse multimodal scenarios and real-time, on-device applications. 
*   •Fully Open-source Resources: To promote reproducibility and facilitate further research, we release all model weights, datasets, code, and a mobile application showcasing inference on a smartphone. 

2 Smoller Model Architecture
----------------------------

We systematically explore design choices for small multimodal models based on the architecture in Figure[2](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#S0.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ SmolVLM: Redefining small and efficient multimodal models"), where encoded images are pooled and projected into a SmolLM2 backbone. We first analyze optimal compute allocation, showing smaller vision encoders complement compact LMs (§[2.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#S2.SS1 "2.1 How to assign compute between vision and language towers? ‣ 2 Smoller Model Architecture ‣ SmolVLM: Redefining small and efficient multimodal models")). Extending context length enables higher image resolutions at minimal overhead (§[2.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#S2.SS2 "2.2 How can we efficiently pass the images to the Language Model? ‣ 2 Smoller Model Architecture ‣ SmolVLM: Redefining small and efficient multimodal models")), and pixel shuffling reduces visual tokens further. Finally, we efficiently handle high-resolution images and videos via document-specific image splitting and targeted token compression (§[2.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#S2.SS3 "2.3 How can we efficiently encode images and videos? ‣ 2 Smoller Model Architecture ‣ SmolVLM: Redefining small and efficient multimodal models")). Together, these approaches yield a unified, performant, and cost-effective recipe for tiny LMMs.

### 2.1 How to assign compute between vision and language towers?

VLMs utilize vision encoders (see Figure[2](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#S0.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ SmolVLM: Redefining small and efficient multimodal models")) to generate ‘vision tokens’ that are then fed into an LM. We investigate optimal capacity allocation between vision encoders and language models (LMs) in compact VLMs. Specifically, we pair three SmolLM2 variants (135 135 135 135 M, 360 360 360 360 M, and 1.7 1.7 1.7 1.7 B parameters) with two SigLIP encoders: a compact 93 93 93 93 M SigLIP-B/16 16 16 16 and a larger 428 428 428 428 M SigLIP-SO 400 400 400 400 M. Typically, larger VLMs disproportionately allocate parameters to the LM; however, as the LM is scaled down, this is no longer the case.

Figure[3](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#S2.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 2 Smoller Model Architecture ‣ SmolVLM: Redefining small and efficient multimodal models") (left) confirms that performance declines significantly when using a large encoder with the smallest LM (135 135 135 135 M), highlighting an inefficient encoder-LM balance. At an intermediate LM scale (360 360 360 360 M), the larger encoder improves performance by 11.6 11.6 11.6 11.6%, yet this comes with a substantial 66 66 66 66% increase in parameters, making the compact encoder preferable. Only at the largest LM scale (1.7 1.7 1.7 1.7 B), the larger encoder represents just a 10 10 10 10% parameter increase.

### 2.2 How can we efficiently pass the images to the Language Model?

Following Laurençon et al. ([2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib38)), we adopt a self-attention architecture in which visual tokens from the vision encoder are concatenated with textual tokens and jointly processed by a language model (e.g., FROMAGe(Koh et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib35)), BLIP-2 2 2 2(Li et al., [2023a](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib41))). This design requires significantly more context than the 2 2 2 2 k-token limit used in SmolLM 2 2 2 2, as a single 512×512 512 512 512\times 512 512 × 512 image encoded with SigLIP-B/16 16 16 16 requires 1024 1024 1024 1024 tokens. To address this, we extended the context capacity by increasing the RoPE base from 10 10 10 10 k to 273 273 273 273 k, following Liu et al. ([2024c](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib52)), and fine-tuned the model on a mix of long-context data (Dolma books (Soldaini et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib76)), The Stack (Kocetkov et al., [2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib34))) and short-context sources (FineWeb-Edu (Penedo et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib69)), DCLM (Li et al., [2024a](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib40)), and math from SmolLM2).

While fine-tuning was stable at 16 16 16 16 k tokens for the 1.7 1.7 1.7 1.7 B LM, smaller models (135M, 360 360 360 360 M) struggled beyond 8 8 8 8 k. Experiments with our 2.2 2.2 2.2 2.2 B SmolVLM confirmed consistent performance gains up to 16 16 16 16 k tokens (Figure[3](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#S2.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 2 Smoller Model Architecture ‣ SmolVLM: Redefining small and efficient multimodal models"), middle). Accordingly, we adopt a 16 16 16 16 k-token context for SmolVLM and an 8 8 8 8 k-token limit for smaller variants.

Extending the context window alone is not sufficient. Recent VLMs (e.g., MM1(McKinzie et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib65)), MiniCPM-V(Yao et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib88)), InternVL(Chen et al., [2024c](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib16))) combine the self-attention architecture with token compression techniques(Zohar et al., [2024b](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib98); Laurençon et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib39)) to fit longer sequences efficiently and reduce computational overhead.

One particularly effective compression method is pixel shuffle (space-to-depth), initially proposed for super-resolution tasks(Shi et al., [2016](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib73)) and recently adopted by Idefics3. Pixel shuffle rearranges spatial features into additional channels, reducing spatial resolution but increasing representational density (Figure[4](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#S2.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 2.3 How can we efficiently encode images and videos? ‣ 2 Smoller Model Architecture ‣ SmolVLM: Redefining small and efficient multimodal models")). This reduces the total number of visual tokens by a factor of r 2 superscript 𝑟 2 r^{2}italic_r start_POSTSUPERSCRIPT 2 end_POSTSUPERSCRIPT, where r 𝑟 r italic_r is the shuffle ratio. However, higher ratios collapse larger spatial regions into single tokens, impairing tasks requiring precise localization, such as OCR. Models like InternVL and Idefics 3 3 3 3 use r=2 𝑟 2 r=2 italic_r = 2 to balance compression and spatial fidelity. In contrast, our experiments (Figure[3](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#S2.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 2 Smoller Model Architecture ‣ SmolVLM: Redefining small and efficient multimodal models"), right) show that smaller VLMs benefit from more aggressive compression (r=4 𝑟 4 r=4 italic_r = 4) as the reduced token count eases attention overhead and improves long-context modeling.

### 2.3 How can we efficiently encode images and videos?

Balancing token allocation between images and videos is crucial for efficient multimodal modeling: images benefit from higher resolution and more tokens to retain fidelity, whereas videos typically require fewer tokens per frame to handle longer sequences efficiently.

To achieve this, we successfully adopted an image-splitting strategy inspired by UReader(Ye et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib89)) and SPHINX(Lin et al., [2023b](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib48)), where high-resolution images are divided into multiple sub-images along with a downsized version of the original. This approach proved effective in maintaining image quality without excessive computational overhead. For videos, however, we found that strategies such as frame averaging, inspired by Liu et al. ([2024f](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib55)), negatively impacted performance. As shown in Figure[3](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#S2.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 2 Smoller Model Architecture ‣ SmolVLM: Redefining small and efficient multimodal models") (right), combining multiple frames significantly degraded OpenCompass-Video results, particularly at higher averaging factors (2 2 2 2, 4 4 4 4, 8 8 8 8). Consequently, frame averaging was excluded from SmolVLM’s final design, and video frames were instead rescaled to the resolution of the image encoder.

3 Smol Instruction Tuning
-------------------------

Smol instruction tuning requires careful vision (§[3.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#S3.SS1 "3.1 Learned Tokens vs. String ‣ 3 Smol Instruction Tuning ‣ SmolVLM: Redefining small and efficient multimodal models")) and text tokenization (§[3.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#S3.SS2 "3.2 Structured Text Prompts and Media Segmentation ‣ 3 Smol Instruction Tuning ‣ SmolVLM: Redefining small and efficient multimodal models")), alongside unified methods for multimodal modeling under tight compute constraints. Learned positional tokens and structured prompts stabilize training and improve OCR, but data composition remains crucial: reusing LLM instruction datasets negatively impacts small VLMs (§[3.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#S3.SS3 "3.3 Impact of Text Data Reuse from LLM-SFT ‣ 3 Smol Instruction Tuning ‣ SmolVLM: Redefining small and efficient multimodal models")), excessive Chain-of-Thought data overwhelms limited capacity (§[3.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#S3.SS4 "3.4 Optimizing Chain-of-Thought Integration for Compact Models ‣ 3 Smol Instruction Tuning ‣ SmolVLM: Redefining small and efficient multimodal models")), and moderate video sequence lengths balance efficiency and performance (§[3.5](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#S3.SS5 "3.5 Impact of Video Sequence Length on Model Performance ‣ 3 Smol Instruction Tuning ‣ SmolVLM: Redefining small and efficient multimodal models")). Collectively, these insights highlight targeted strategies essential for effectively scaling multimodal instruction tuning to SmolVLMs.

### 3.1 Learned Tokens vs. String

A primary design consideration in SmolVLM involves encoding split sub-image positions effectively. Initially, we attempted to use simple string tokens (e.g., <row_1_col_2>), which caused early training plateaus—termed the “OCR loss plague”—characterized by sudden loss drops without corresponding improvements in OCR performance (Figure[5](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#S3.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 3.1 Learned Tokens vs. String ‣ 3 Smol Instruction Tuning ‣ SmolVLM: Redefining small and efficient multimodal models"), left and middle).

To address instability during training, we introduced positional tokens, significantly improving training convergence and reducing stalls. Although larger models were relatively robust to using raw string positions, smaller models benefited substantially from positional tokens, achieving notably higher OCR accuracy and improved generalization across tasks. Figure[5](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#S3.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 3.1 Learned Tokens vs. String ‣ 3 Smol Instruction Tuning ‣ SmolVLM: Redefining small and efficient multimodal models") (center) shows that learned positional tokens consistently outperform naive string positions on multiple image and text benchmarks. Additionally, Figure[5](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#S3.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 3.1 Learned Tokens vs. String ‣ 3 Smol Instruction Tuning ‣ SmolVLM: Redefining small and efficient multimodal models") (right) illustrates that models leveraging learned tokens consistently score higher in both OpenCompass-Image and OpenCompass-Video evaluations, underscoring the effectiveness of structured positional tokenization in compact multimodal models.

### 3.2 Structured Text Prompts and Media Segmentation

We evaluated how system prompts and explicit media intro/outro prefixes incrementally improve SmolVLM’s performance on image (left) and video (right) benchmarks, as shown in Figure[6](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#S3.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ 3.3 Impact of Text Data Reuse from LLM-SFT ‣ 3 Smol Instruction Tuning ‣ SmolVLM: Redefining small and efficient multimodal models"). Each violin plot represents three checkpoints for a given configuration.

System Prompts. We prepend concise instructions to clarify task objectives and reduce ambiguity during zero-shot inference. For example, conversational datasets utilize prompts like “You are a useful conversational assistant,” whereas vision-focused tasks employ “You are a visual agent and should provide concise answers.” The second violin plot in each subplot (Fig.[6](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#S3.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ 3.3 Impact of Text Data Reuse from LLM-SFT ‣ 3 Smol Instruction Tuning ‣ SmolVLM: Redefining small and efficient multimodal models")) illustrates clear performance improvements from incorporating these system prompts, particularly evident in image-centric tasks.

Media Intro/Outro Tokens. To clearly demarcate visual content, we introduce textual markers around image and video segments (e.g., “Here is an image…” and “Here are N 𝑁 N italic_N frames sampled from a video…”). The outro tokens then transition back to textual instructions (e.g., “Given this image/video…”). The third violin indicates that this strategy substantially boosts performance on video tasks—where confusion between multiple frames is more likely—and still yields measurable improvements on image tasks.

Masking User Prompts Drawing on techniques from Allal et al. ([2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib4)), we explore user-prompt masking during supervised fine-tuning as a way to reduce overfitting. The right violin plot in Figure[6](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#S3.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ 3.3 Impact of Text Data Reuse from LLM-SFT ‣ 3 Smol Instruction Tuning ‣ SmolVLM: Redefining small and efficient multimodal models") shows that masking user queries (orange) yields improved performance in both image and video tasks, compared to the unmasked baseline (blue). This effect is significantly pronounced in multimodal QA, where questions are often repetitive and can be trivially memorized by the model. Masking thus forces SmolVLM to rely on task-related content rather than superficial repetition, promoting better generalization.

### 3.3 Impact of Text Data Reuse from LLM-SFT

A seemingly intuitive practice is to reuse text data from the final supervised fine-tuning stages of large language models, anticipating in-distribution prompts and higher-quality linguistic inputs. However, Figure[7](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#S3.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ 3.5 Impact of Video Sequence Length on Model Performance ‣ 3 Smol Instruction Tuning ‣ SmolVLM: Redefining small and efficient multimodal models") (left) shows that incorporating LLM-SFT text data (_SmolTalk_) can degrade performance in smaller multimodal architectures by as much as 3.7% in video tasks and 6.5% in image tasks. We attribute this negative transfer to reduced data diversity, which outweighs any benefits of reusing text. In keeping with Zohar et al. ([2024b](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib98)), we therefore maintain a strict 14% text proportion in our training mix. These findings highlight the importance of a carefully balanced data pipeline, rather than direct adoption of large-scale SFT text for small-scale multimodal models.

### 3.4 Optimizing Chain-of-Thought Integration for Compact Models

Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting, which exposes models to explicit reasoning steps during training, generally enhances reasoning capabilities in large models. However, its effect on smaller multimodal architectures remains unclear. To investigate this, we varied the proportion of CoT data integrated into the Mammoth dataset (Yue et al., [2024b](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib91)), covering text, image, and video tasks. Figure[7](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#S3.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ 3.5 Impact of Video Sequence Length on Model Performance ‣ 3 Smol Instruction Tuning ‣ SmolVLM: Redefining small and efficient multimodal models") (middle) shows that incorporating a minimal fraction (0.02–0.05%) of CoT examples slightly improved performance, but higher proportions markedly degraded results, especially in image tasks. These observations suggest that excessive reasoning-oriented textual data can overwhelm the limited capacity of smaller VLMs, thereby compromising their visual representation capabilities. Consequently, compact models benefit most from very sparse inclusion of CoT data rather than the extensive use typically beneficial in larger-scale architectures.

### 3.5 Impact of Video Sequence Length on Model Performance

Increasing video duration during training offers richer temporal context but comes at a greater computational cost. To identify an optimal duration, we trained SmolVLM on average video lengths ranging from 1.5 to 3.5 minutes. Figure[7](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#S3.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ 3.5 Impact of Video Sequence Length on Model Performance ‣ 3 Smol Instruction Tuning ‣ SmolVLM: Redefining small and efficient multimodal models") (right) demonstrates clear performance improvements for both video and image benchmarks as video durations approached approximately 3.5 minutes, likely due to more effective cross-modal feature learning. Extending video duration beyond 3.5 minutes yielded minimal further gains, indicating diminishing returns relative to the added computational expense. Thus, moderately extending video sequences enhances performance significantly in smaller models, whereas overly long sequences do not proportionally justify their computational cost.

We construct three variants of SmolVLM, tailored to different computational environments:

*   •SmolVLM-𝟓𝟎𝟎 500\bm{500}bold_500 M: A mid-range model with the same 93M SigLIP-B/16 16 16 16 paired with the larger SmolLM 2 2 2 2-360 360 360 360 M. Balancing memory efficiency and performance, it is suitable for moderate-resource edge devices.

### 4.1 Training Data

Model training proceeds in two stages: (1) a vision stage, and (2) a video stage. The vision training stage uses a new mixture of the datasets used in Laurençon et al. ([2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib39)), to which we added MathWriting (Gervais et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib26)). The mixture was balanced to emphasize visual and structured data interpretation while maintaining the focus on reasoning and problem-solving capabilities. The visual components comprise document understanding, captioning, and visual question answering (including 2 2 2 2% dedicated to multi-image reasoning), chart understanding, table understanding, and visual reasoning tasks. The video fine-tuning stage maintains 14 14 14 14% of text data and 33 33 33 33% of video to achieve optimal performance, following the learnings of Zohar et al. ([2024b](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib98)). ### 4.5 Downstream Applications

Beyond our own evaluations, SmolVLM has seen adoption in various downstream applications developed by the broader research community, emphasizing its efficiency in real-world, resource-constrained scenarios.

#### ColSmolVLM: On-Device Multimodal Inference.

ColSmolVLM utilizes the smaller SmolVLM variants (256 256 256 256 M and 500 500 500 500 M parameters) designed explicitly for on-device deployment, as detailed in recent work by Hugging Face(Faysse et al., [2024b](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib23)). These compact models enable efficient multimodal inference directly on mobile devices, consumer laptops, and even within browser-based environments, significantly lowering computational demands and operational costs.

#### Smol Docling: Ultra-Compact Document Processing.

Smol Docling is an ultra-compact 256M-parameter variant of SmolVLM, optimized explicitly for end-to-end multimodal document conversion tasks(Nassar et al., [2025b](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib67)). By employing specialized representations known as DocTags, Smol Docling efficiently captures content, context, and spatial relationships across diverse document types, including business documents, academic papers, and patents. Its compact architecture maintains competitive performance with considerably larger VLMs, highlighting its suitability for deployment in scenarios with computational constraints.

#### BioVQA: Biomedical Visual Question Answering.

BioVQA leverages SmolVLM’s compact and efficient architecture to address visual question answering tasks within the biomedical domain(Lozano et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib58)). Small-scale SmolVLM models have demonstrated promising capabilities in interpreting medical images, assisting healthcare professionals by providing accurate answers to clinical questions based on visual data. This capability is particularly valuable in healthcare settings where quick, reliable image interpretation is critical, yet computational resources may be limited.

5 Related Work

### 5.1 First-Generation Vision-Language Models

Early multimodal models achieved significant progress primarily by scaling parameters, but their high computational demands limited practical deployment. For instance, Flamingo(Alayrac et al., [2022b](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib3)), an 80 80 80 80 B-parameter Vision-Language Model (VLM), integrated a frozen 70 70 70 70 B-parameter LM(Hoffmann et al., [2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib29)) with a vision encoder employing gated cross-attention and a Perceiver Resampler(Jaegle et al., [2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib32)) for efficient token compression. Despite state-of-the-art few-shot capabilities without task-specific fine-tuning, Flamingo’s large scale posed significant deployment challenges.

Hugging Face’s Idefics(Laurençon et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib37)) adopted Flamingo’s architecture, offering models at both 9B and 80B parameters, further exemplifying the approach of large-scale multimodal training. In contrast, BLIP-2(Li et al., [2023a](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib41)) proposed a more parameter-efficient, modular design by freezing both the vision encoder and language model, introducing instead a lightweight Query Transformer (Q-Former) that translates visual features into language-compatible tokens. This approach significantly reduced trainable parameters, surpassing Flamingo’s performance on VQA tasks(Antol et al., [2015](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib5); Goyal et al., [2017](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib27)) with roughly 54 times fewer trainable parameters, thus paving the way toward more efficient multimodal architectures.

Similarly, LLaVA (Large Language-and-Vision Assistant)(Liu et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib49)) connected a pretrained CLIP(Radford et al., [2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib70)) ViT image encoder to a LLaMA/Vicuna language backbone(Touvron et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib81); Zheng et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib95)), fine-tuning the combined model on instruction-following datasets. Resulting in a 13B-parameter multimodal chatbot with GPT-4V-like capabilities(Achiam et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib1)), LLaVA achieved notable visual conversational performance. However, despite being smaller and faster than Flamingo, it still demands substantial GPU memory for real-time interaction and inherits the limitations of the underlying language model’s context window (typically 2048 tokens).

Recent research has actively explored various design choices, training strategies, and data configurations to enhance Vision-Language Models (VLMs). For instance, Idefics2(Laurençon et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib38)) introduced architectural and training-data improvements compared to its predecessor, advancing open-source VLM capabilities. Concurrently, Cambrian1(Tong et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib80)) examined fundamental design principles and scaling behaviors, aiming for more efficient architectures. Projects like Eagle(Shi et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib72)) and its successor Eagle2(Li et al., [2025b](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib46)) have optimized specific architectural components, targeting improved performance and efficiency. Additionally, recent efforts such as Apollo(Zohar et al., [2024b](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib98)) extend multimodal architectures from static images to video understanding, further enriching the diversity of approaches.

### 5.2 Efficiency-Focused Vision-Language Models

Larger models, such as InternVL (Chen et al., [2024c](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib16), [b](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib15)) and Qwen-VL (Bai et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib7), [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib8); Wang et al., [2024a](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib82)), introduced architectural innovations for improved computational efficiency. InternVL aligns a 6B-parameter vision transformer (ViT) with an 8B-parameter language "middleware," forming a 14B-parameter model that achieves state-of-the-art results across multiple vision and multimodal tasks. This balanced architecture narrows the modality gap, enabling robust multimodal perception and generation capabilities. Similarly, Qwen-VL integrates a Qwen language model with specialized visual modules, leveraging captioned bounding-box data to enhance visual grounding and text recognition capabilities. Despite its strong multilingual and multimodal performance, Qwen-VL generates exceptionally long token sequences for high-resolution inputs, increasing memory requirements.

On the smaller end, models like PaliGemma, Moondream2, and MiniCPM-V demonstrate impressive multimodal capabilities within constrained parameter budgets. PaliGemma (Team et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib79)), with just 3B parameters (400M vision encoder from SigLIP-So (Zhai et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib92)) and 2B Gemma language model), effectively covers a wide range of multimodal tasks. However, its condensed visual interface can limit detailed visual analysis. Moondream2, at merely 1.8B parameters, pairs SigLIP visual features with Microsoft’s Phi-1.5 language model (Li et al., [2023b](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib44)), showcasing competitive performance on tasks such as image description, OCR, counting, and classification, ideal for edge and mobile applications. MiniCPM-V (Hu et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib31)), specifically designed for on-device scenarios, integrates a 400M vision encoder and a 7.5B language model via a perceiver-style adapter. This compact model notably achieves GPT-4V-level performance on selected benchmarks. Deepseek VL and Deepseek VL2 (Lu et al., [2024a](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib59); Wu et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib84)), spanning 2–7B and 4–27B parameters respectively, further illustrate the growing focus on efficient yet powerful multimodal models suitable for resource-constrained environments. Collectively, these models demonstrate the increasing feasibility of deploying effective, real-time multimodal AI in practical scenarios.

### 5.3 Multimodal Tokenization and Compression Strategies

Efficient tokenization significantly reduces computational and memory demands in Vision-Language Models (VLMs). Early methods, encoding every pixel or patch individually, resulted in lengthy sequences—196 tokens for a 224×\times×224 image at 16×\times×16 resolution. Recent strategies compress visual data while preserving essential details. Learned modules like Perceiver Resamplers (Jaegle et al., [2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib32)) used by Flamingo and Idefics2 (Alayrac et al., [2022b](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib3); Laurençon et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib38)), and BLIP-2’s Q-Former (Li et al., [2023a](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib41)), compress inputs into a small set of latent tokens. While effective in shortening sequences, these methods may limit performance on fine-grained tasks like OCR (Singh et al., [2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib75); Biten et al., [2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib10)). Spatial compression via patch pooling and pixel shuffle is increasingly popular. InternVL v1.5 and Idefics3 (Chen et al., [2024c](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib16), [b](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib15); Laurençon et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib37)) use 2×\times×2 pixel-shuffle, reducing token counts fourfold while maintaining OCR capability. Models like Qwen-VL-2 (Wang et al., [2024a](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib82)) adopt multi-scale representations and selective token dropping via convolutional and Transformer modules. Adaptive methods, such as image tiling in UReader and DocOwl, dynamically adjust token counts based on task complexity, sacrificing some global context.

### 5.4 Video-Capable Vision-Language Models

Extending vision-language models (VLMs) from images to videos significantly increases complexity due to temporal dimensions, expanding token counts and computational demands. Early models, such as Video-LLaVA (Lin et al., [2023a](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib47)), unified image and video training, aligning video frame features with static images and substantially outperforming predecessors like Video-ChatGPT (Maaz et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib62)) on benchmarks including MSRVTT (Xu et al., [2016](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib85)), MSVD (Chen and Dolan, [2011](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib12)), TGIF (Li et al., [2016](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib45)), and ActivityNet (Caba Heilbron et al., [2015](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib11)). Meanwhile, Video-STaR(Zohar et al., [2024a](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib97)) introduced the first self-training approach that leverages existing labeled video datasets for instruction tuning of Large Multimodal Models.

Recent models enhance efficiency and effectiveness in handling long-form video content. Temporal Preference Optimization (TPO) (Li et al., [2025a](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib43)) employs self-training with localized and comprehensive temporal grounding, improving benchmarks like LongVideoBench, MLVU, and Video-MME. Oryx MLLM (Liu et al., [2024g](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib56)) dynamically compresses visual tokens via its OryxViT encoder, balancing efficiency and precision across tasks. VideoAgent (Wang et al., [2024b](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib83)) models long-form video understanding as a decision-making process, utilizing a large language model (LLM) as an agent to identify and compile crucial information for question answering iteratively. VideoLLaMA3 (Zhang et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib93)) adapts its vision encoder for variable resolutions and uses multi-task fine-tuning to enhance video comprehension. Video-XL (Shu et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib74)) introduces Visual Summarization Tokens (VST) and curriculum learning for efficient handling of hour-scale videos. Similarly, Kangaroo (Liu et al., [2024b](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib51)) utilizes curriculum training to scale input resolution and frame count progressively, achieving top performance on diverse benchmarks.

Apollo (Zohar et al., [2024b](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib98)) recently made an in-depth exploration of Video-LMMs and showed the architecture and training schedule that most affect performance. In so doing, it showed the remarkable efficiency gains that can be made during training and inference. Apollo achieved state-of-the-art results with modest parameter sizes on benchmarks such as LongVideoBench, MLVU, and Video-MME (Zhou et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib96); Fu et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib24)).

6 Conclusion
------------

We introduced SmolVLM, a family of memory-efficient Vision-Language Models ranging from 256M to 2.2B parameters. Remarkably, even our smallest variant requires less than 1GB of GPU memory yet surpasses state-of-the-art 80B-parameter models from just 18 months ago(Laurençon et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05299v1#bib.bib37)). Our findings emphasize a critical insight: scaling down large VLM architectures optimized under resource-rich conditions results in disproportionately high memory demands during inference with little advantage over specialized architectures. By contrast, SmolVLM’s design philosophy explicitly prioritizes compact architectural innovations, aggressive but careful tokenization methods, and efficient training strategies, enabling powerful multimodal capabilities at a fraction of the computational cost.

All model weights, training datasets, and training code are publicly released to encourage reproducibility, transparency, and continued innovation. We hope SmolVLM will inspire the next generation of lightweight, efficient VLMs, unlocking new possibilities for real-time multimodal inference with minimal power consumption.
